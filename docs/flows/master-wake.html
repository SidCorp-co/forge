master-wake — what makes a box look at a project, and what happens when the signal is lost

Nothing pushed work to a box until ISS-933: daemon/master.rs polled every project it served on a 30-second timer, and that interval was the latency from an issue arriving to an agent touching it. This is the push, the timer that stayed underneath it, and the third trigger that exists only because the transport drops frames in silence. Owner: packages/core/src/ws/master-wake.ts, packages/runner/crates/forge-runner-core/src/daemon/master.rs, .../daemon/mod.rs, .../transport/ws.rs.

Three triggers, one sweep

The design rule is that there is exactly one path from “something might be there” to “the pool was read”. A wake is not a dispatcher: it carries no job, no token and no decision. It says look now, and the box then reads the pool through the same call its timer already used and decides for itself.

The timer is the floor and it must stay. ws/rooms.ts:publish is fire-and-forget: it returns 0 for a room with no subscriber and skips any socket that is not OPEN, with no buffer, no queue and no replay. A wake published while a box's websocket is down is gone, and nothing anywhere records that it happened. With the timer, that drop costs 30 seconds of latency; without it, the drop costs the work — it sits forever with nothing reporting why. The wake is a latency cut layered on a poll, never a replacement for it.

The dropped-wake hole, and the two things that cover it

The reconnect catch-up is the second half of that defence, and it exists because the gap has a shape the timer alone does not cover well: a box that was disconnected for ten minutes missed every wake in that window, and on reconnect it would otherwise wait out a fresh poll interval before looking. One pool read on the way back up turns that into nothing.

The catch-up is deliberately the same wake path with a different trigger, not a second reader. A separate “reconnect handler that reads the pool” would be a second dispatcher on the box, and the two would drift the moment either grew a condition. The transport itself knows nothing about masters or pools: it reports that its socket came up and stops there, and mod.rs is the one place that decides what that means.

Coalescing — why the channel holds exactly one

Core publishes one frame per issue arrival. Promoting five drafts, or a batch closing, delivers five frames in about as many milliseconds. The wake channel has capacity 1 and drops the rest on purpose.

flowchart LR
  A["5 master.wake frames
within ~5ms"] --> B{"wake_tx.try_send"}
  B -- "1st: channel empty" --> C["queued"]
  B -- "2nd-5th: channel full" --> D["dropped, logged at debug
'coalesced — a sweep is already pending'"]
  C --> E{"last sweep
less than WAKE_FLOOR ago?"}
  E -- yes --> F["sleep the remainder
— waits, never discards"]
  E -- no --> G
  F --> G["sweep: reads the WHOLE pool"]
  D -.->|"covered by"| G
A dropped frame costs nothing because the sweep that follows reads the entire pool rather than the issue the frame named — so one sweep covers every wake that arrived while it was pending. try_send rather than an awaited send matters for a second reason: this arm runs on the one frame loop that also carries job.cancel, and blocking it on a full wake channel would stall a kill behind a sweep that was already going to cover the wake.
WAKE_FLOOR must stay below POLL_INTERVAL. The whole point of the push is to beat the timer; a floor at or above 30s would make every wake wait longer than doing nothing. a_wake_cuts_latency_rather_than_adding_it is the assertion that fails if someone raises it.

What is NOT here yet, and why the diagram stops where it does

ISS-933 describes four waves. This flow is wave 1 only — core wakes the box, and the box looks. The master's lifecycle beyond that is unchanged: it is still a resident session prompted per pass, still supervised by supervise, still killed on a silence ceiling, and every run is still a jobs row claimed from the pool.

The close-the-loop half is the known hole, and it is unowned today. When a run ends, three things must happen: its agent_sessions row reaches terminal, its worktree is removed, and its tracker lease is returned. Nothing performs them and nothing checks they happened — it rests on the master remembering all three. Measured on forge-dev 2026-09-06: a master reported a loop closed having done one and a half of the three, and the third is not merely forgettable but currently impossible — forge claim gates on wall clock rather than liveness, so a lease held by a dead pid refuses every claim for its full duration (forge-plugin ISS-602). Wave 2 multiplies this by one loop per run. Any automatic reaper must consult the lease rather than cwd, mtime or “no tmux session by that name” — a session working a second worktree is held from a cwd that is not inside it — and the lease cannot be that authority until it can tell a live holder from a dead one.

Read this before

Adding a status to MASTER_WAKE_STATUSES — the driver's mid-run statuses (in_progress, needs_info) are an issue a master already handed to a run, so waking on them turns one issue's lifecycle into a burst of pool reads that can find nothing new by construction. Filtering devicesServing on runners.status — declining work is the box's act, not core's, and master.rs:accepts_new_work carries the guard saying core adding the same filter would hide work from a master rather than let the box decline it. Removing the timer, the catch-up, or either one's guard — each covers a different half of a transport that loses frames without saying so. And widening the wake channel past capacity 1: that is not “losing fewer wakes”, it is one redundant full sweep per queued frame.
